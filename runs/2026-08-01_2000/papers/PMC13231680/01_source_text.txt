The emergence and global spread of New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase-1 (NDM-1) pose a severe threat to the efficacy of carbapenems, which are considered the last line of defense against infections caused by multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria. In this study, through virtual screening and enzyme activity inhibition assays, phthalylsulfacetamide (PSA) was identified as a compound exhibiting inhibitory activity against NDM-1. PSA dose-dependently inhibited NDM-1, with a 50% inhibitory concentration (IC 50 ) of 15.4 ± 0.3 µM and an inhibition rate (IR%) of 89.2%. In vitro antibacterial activity assays, confirmed that PSA effectively restored the antibacterial activity of meropenem (MEM) against NDM-1-positive Escherichia. coli ( E. coli ), reducing the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of MEM by 32-fold and demonstrating rapid bactericidal effects within 3 h. The combination of PSA and MEM had a significant synergistic effect with a fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) of 0.156. In vivo, the combination therapy significantly reduced the bacterial load, decreased inflammatory cytokine levels, alleviated histopathological damage and increased survival rates in a murine systemic infection model. Molecular dynamics simulation and site-directed mutagenesis revealed that PSA stably binds to the active hydrolysis center of NDM-1, interacts with Zn 2+ , and strongly interacts with the key amino acid residues Val73 and His122. Furthermore, the observed rapid bactericidal activity suggests that PSA’s mechanism of action may extend beyond NDM-1 inhibition. Molecular docking analysis indicates that PSA may potentially interact with LpxC, a key enzyme involved in lipopolysaccharide synthesis; however, this observation remains preliminary and requires further experimental validation. These results highlight PSA as a promising therapeutic agent against NDM-1-mediated carbapenem resistance. Penicillin and cephalosporins are the most commonly used first-line drugs for the treatment of drug-resistant Escherichia coli ( E. coli ) infection in veterinary clinics [ 3 ]. Carbapenems such as meropenem (MEM) are known as the “last line of defense” for treating multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae infections and are listed as highly important antibacterial drugs for human medicine by the World Health Organization (WHO) [ 4 ]. No country in the world has approved the use of MEM in animals, but many animal-derived carbapenem-resistant E. coli strains have emerged, posing a great challenge to the treatment of colibacillosis [ 5 , 6 ]. β-lactamases (BLs) secreted by bacteria can efficiently hydrolyze a variety of β-lactam antibiotics, which is the main mechanism of β-lactam antibiotic failure and bacterial drug resistance [ 7 ]. BLs can be divided into serine β-lactamases (SBLs) and metallo β-lactamases (MBLs) according to their different amino acid sequences and mechanisms [ 8 ]. Among the mechanisms causing gram-negative bacterial infections, subclass B1 MBLs have become the number one threat to clinical β-lactam antibiotic therapy because of their extensive hydrolysis substrate profile, among which New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase-1 (NDM-1) is the most concerning [ 9 – 11 ]. Bacteria expressing NDM-1 can stably and efficiently hydrolyze all β-lactam antibiotics except monocyclic antibiotics, including broad-spectrum cephalosporins and carbapenems [ 12 ]. Further research revealed that bla NDM−1 was localized on IncA/c, IncF, IncX 1 and other plasmids, and could spread and transfer among different bacteria and strains, mainly E. coli , Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae [ 13 ]. The bla NDM−1 can even spread rapidly among animals, the environment, and humans through biological media [ 14 ]. Selective pressure from antibiotic overuse continues to drive the evolution of NDM-1, with over 40 variants identified [ 15 ]. Bacteria that produce the NDM-1 enzyme are called “superbugs” because of their extensive drug resistance and transmission risk. To combat these “superdrug-resistant bacteria”, the development of new antibiotics or antibiotic alternatives is urgently needed [ 16 , 17 ]. However, the development of new antibiotics is hindered by several disadvantages, including high costs, extended timelines, and a significant rate of rediscovery [ 18 , 19 ]. This challenge is particularly pronounced for gram-negative bacteria, which possess outer membrane permeability barriers that complicate the development of effective new antibiotics [ 20 ]. In comparison, the development of new MBL inhibitors that target resistance mechanisms, combined with β-lactam antibiotics, presents a low-cost, safe, and efficient approach to enhance the antibacterial activity of existing antibiotics [ 21 ]. This strategy is currently among the most effective methods for addressing “superbug” infections [ 22 ]. Among antibacterial synergists, SBL inhibitors have been more successfully studied. At present, the SBL inhibitors approved by the FDA include clavulanic acid, sulbactam and tazobactam [ 23 ]. Unlike SBLs, MBLs are zinc ion-dependent enzymes that cannot be inhibited by SBLs inhibitor [ 24 ]. Owing to the lack of effective NDM-1 inhibitors for clinical use, screening for specific NDM-1 enzyme inhibitors is essential for the treatment of multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacterial infections. Virtual screening based on molecular docking is a method that simulates and computationally evaluates the interactions between the three-dimensional structure of target proteins and small molecules. It assesses the binding affinity and conformation of various compounds with the target protein to identify potential active candidates. The process mainly involves receptor modeling, preprocessing of the small-molecule library, docking and scoring, and post-processing of hit compounds, with docking and scoring being the core steps. In this study, the Glide module of the Schrödinger software was employed for docking. This module is characterized by fast processing speed and high accuracy, providing reliable support for the efficient discovery of novel NDM-1 inhibitors [ 25 ]. From a diverse ligand compound library, phthalylsulfacetamide (PSA) was identified as a promising candidate. PSA is a broad-spectrum bacteriostatic agent classified as a sulfonamide [ 26 ]. Upon oral administration, PSA is decomposed in the intestine, resulting in an antibacterial effect [ 27 ]. This agent exhibits therapeutic efficacy against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacterial infections. Clinically, PSA is primarily utilized for the treatment of bacillary dysentery and diarrhea and for the prevention of infections before and after enteritis and intestinal surgery [ 26 ]. This study aimed to discover novel NDM-1 inhibitors targeting its zinc active site using computational screening. PSA was identified as an effective NDM-1 inhibitor, demonstrating synergistic antibacterial activity with MEM in vitro and in vivo, and its mechanism was elucidated. This work provides a potential strategy and foundation for developing specific inhibitors against NDM-1-mediated resistance and treating drug-resistant bacterial infections. Materials and methods Molecular docking The crystal structure of an NDM-1-ligand complex expressed in E. coli was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org/ ) (PDB ID: 4EY2) as the initial structure for molecular docking. Small-molecule ligands were selected from the BioActive Compound Library (Target Mol, USA). Both the NDM-1 protein structure and the small-molecule compounds were preprocessed using the Glide module within the Schrödinger software package. Importantly, the Zn²⁺ ions present in the crystal structure were retained during protein preparation, and their coordination geometry was preserved as defined in the original structure. During receptor grid generation, the docking site was centered on the Zn²⁺-containing active site to ensure appropriate sampling of ligands capable of interacting with the metal center. In the Glide docking protocol, the Zn²⁺ ion was treated as an integral part of the receptor, allowing potential metal–ligand coordination interactions to be considered during scoring. Semiflexible docking was then performed between NDM-1 and the ligand library using the Glide and Maestro modules. Potential NDM-1 inhibitors were identified on the basis of docking scores (G-scores, representing semiempirical binding free energy) and compliance with Lipinski’s rule of five for drug-like properties. Based on the LpxC enzyme structure obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 3PS2), the zinc ion site was defined as the active docking site. Subsequently, the aforementioned molecular docking method was employed to assess the binding potential between PSA and LpxC. Determination of the IR% and IC 50 PSA was purchased from Shanghai Taoshu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) with a purity of 97.32%. The chemical structure of PSA was obtained from the PubChem database (CID: 8574). Recombinant NDM-1 enzyme was prepared as described in our previous study [ 28 ], where it was heterologously expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3), purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography, and its purity and enzymatic activity were confirmed prior to use. The NDM-1 inhibition rate (IR, %) was calculated as follows: IR (%) = (1 − Vs/Vn) × 100%, where Vs and Vn represent the enzymatic reaction rates of the PSA and DMSO control, respectively. The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC 50 ) was determined as the concentration of inhibitor that reduced the enzyme activity by 50% and was calculated using SPSS. All experiments were performed in triplicate. The final concentration of DMSO in the reaction system was 1% (v/v), and the same concentration was used in the control group. Determination of the MIC and FICI NDM-1-positive E. coli isolates ( ISAba 125 -bla NDM−1 -ble MBL -Δ trpF ), identified from water samples, were cultured to the logarithmic phase in Cation-Adjusted Mueller-Hinton Broth (CAMHB). The culture was then adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and subsequently diluted in CAMHB to a working concentration of 5 × 10 6 CFU/mL. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using the broth microdilution in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI M07, 2020). Briefly, two-fold serial dilutions of PSA (97.32% pure) and MEM (99.66% pure), ranging from 1 to 2048 µg/mL, were prepared in CAMHB within microtiter plates. Each plate included a growth control (bacteria without drug) and a sterility control (CAMHB only). After incubation at 37℃ for 16 h, bacterial growth was assessed by measuring the optical density at 600 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK). The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration at which growth was fully inhibited. Checkerboard synergy testing of PSA and MEM was performed via microbroth dilution. Synergy was assessed by calculating the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) using the following formula: (MIC of PSA (combined) /MIC of PSA (alone)) + (MIC of MEM (combined) /MIC of MEM (alone)). FICI interpretation: ≤0.5 (synergy), 0.5-1 (additive), 1–4 (indifference), > 4 (antagonism). Triplicate biological replicates were performed. Bacterial growth curves and time-kill curves NDM-1-positive E. coli was cultured to an optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) of 0.3. The bacterial suspension was then aliquoted into six test tubes (5 mL per tube). PSA was added to each tube at concentrations of 0, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 µg/mL. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 6 h, during which OD 600 measurements were taken hourly. The experiment was repeated three times in parallel. The NDM-1-positive E. coli culture was diluted to a concentration of 1 × 10 6 CFU/mL and aliquoted into four test tubes (5 mL per tube). Four experimental groups were established: a blank control group, a PSA group (64 µg/mL), a MEM group (2 µg/mL), and a combination group (2 µg/mL MEM + 64 µg/mL PSA). All the tubes were incubated at 37 °C with constant shaking. At the designated time points (0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11 h), 100 µL of bacterial suspension was collected from each group, plated on MH agar, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h for colony counting. Determination of the effective dose of PSA combined with MEM All animal studies were reviewed and approved by the Special Committee on Scientific Research Ethics of Liao Cheng University (Approval No: 2023022707). We strictly adhered to ethical animal welfare principles and remained subject to the supervision and inspection of the committee at all times. Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–20 g weight) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Before the experiment, all the mice were adaptively raised for 7 days, during which time they had unrestricted access to food and water. Previous studies have indicated that 10 mg/kg is the optimal MEM dose for mice [ 29 ]. Accordingly, we established MEM-PSA combination groups at three dose ratios (1:2, 1:1, and 2:1): (1) high-dose PSA (20 mg/kg) with MEM (10 mg/kg), (2) medium-dose PSA (10 mg/kg) with MEM (10 mg/kg), and (3) low-dose PSA (5 mg/kg) with MEM (10 mg/kg). Following intraperitoneal inoculation with 100 µL of NDM-1-positive E. coli (1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL), the infected mice were randomly assigned to five groups ( n = 5). Different treatments commenced 2 h post-infection, with 100 µL doses administered every 12 h for a total of six administrations. The infection control group received equivalent volumes of normal saline. In accordance with the pharmacokinetic profiles, PSA was delivered via intragastric gavage while MEM was administered subcutaneously [ 30 ]. At 96 h post-infection, the mice were euthanized by gradual CO₂ inhalation (10–30% chamber volume/min) in a sealed chamber to minimize pain from nasal irritation, followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death, and whole blood was then collected. Whole blood was analyzed using a BC-2800Vet veterinary hematology analyzer, with a particular focus on white blood cell (WBC) counts across treatment groups. Tissue samples (liver, spleen, and small intestine) were homogenized for 2 min at 12,000 rpm using an automated tissue grinder. The homogenates were then plated on MEM-resistant selective medium and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Colony counts were log-transformed for comparative analysis of the bacterial load across treatment groups. Serum was isolated by centrifugation at 4 °C and 5000 rpm for 10 min, and the cytokine levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ were quantified according to the instructions of the enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA) kit (Fig. 1 ). After 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, the plates were incubated for 0.5–4 h in the dark. Cell proliferation and PSA safety were assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. In vivo therapeutic experiments Following a one-week acclimation period, 50 6-8-week-old SPF BALB/c mice were randomly divided into five groups ( n = 10). The groups included a normal group (uninfected), an infected control group (saline), a MEM treatment group (10 mg/kg), PSA treatment group (20 mg/kg), and a combined treatment group (10 mg/kg + 20 mg/kg). All groups except normal controls received an intraperitoneal injection of 100 µL of a lethal dose (1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL) of NDM-1-positive E. coli . Treatments were initiated 2 h post-infection (100 µL, q12h×6 doses). Survival was monitored at 24 h intervals for 120 h. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 100 µL of NDM-1-positive E. coli (1 × 10 8 CFU/ml, sublethal concentration) using the same grouping, dose, and administration methods as in the survival experiment. At 96 h post-infection, the clinical symptoms and pathological necropsies of the treatment groups were compared. Following the protocol established in the preliminary dose experiment, we evaluated white blood cell counts; bacterial loads in the liver, spleen, and small intestine; and inflammatory factor levels. Pathological changes in these organs were examined using hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and electron microscopy, while intestinal villus ultrastructure was assessed by uranyl acetate-lead citrate double staining with transmission electron microscopy (TEM). We comprehensively evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of the PSA-MEM combination therapy in mice infected with NDM-1-positive E. coli (Fig. 2 ). Fig. 2 In vivo therapeutic experimental scheme for evaluating the efficacy of PSA and MEM combination therapy Molecular dynamics simulation The PSA-NDM-1 molecular docking complex served as the initial conformation for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. This complex was placed in a TIP3P cubic water box, maintaining a minimum 12 Å water layer from the NDM-1 surface. After assigning the ff03 and gaff force field parameters, we performed energy minimization using the AMBER 20 Sander module with steepest descent followed by conjugate gradient methods. A 150-ns MD simulation was conducted with stepwise heating, and conformations were saved every 1 ps. System equilibration and stability were evaluated through the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the backbone atoms (C, N, and O) using mm_pbsa.pl. The binding free energy was calculated via molecular mechanics generalized Boltzmann surface area (MM/GBSA) using the formula ΔG bind = ΔE vdw + ΔE ele + ΔG GB + ΔG SA –T ΔS . In this formula, ΔE vdw refers to the van der Waals energy, ΔE ele refers to the electrostatic energy, ΔG GB / ΔG SA represents the polar/nonpolar solvation energy, and -T ΔS indicates the entropic contribution at temperature T. MM/GBSA free energy decomposition was performed using the AMBER 20 mm_pbsa.pl script to quantify residue-specific interactions between PSA and NDM-1 and identify key amino acid contributions to the binding free energy of the complex. Site-directed mutagenesis of key amino acids On the basis of the MM/GBSA free energy decomposition results, the key amino acid that binds NDM-1 to PSA was mutated to alanine. Primers for the NDM-1 mutant were designed using the QuikChange method, with sequences provided in Table 1 (mutation sites underlined), and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai). Enzyme inhibition assays were performed according to established methods to compare the inhibitory activity of PSA against wild-type and mutant NDM-1 [ 27 ]. Results Molecular docking analysis Molecular docking studies were conducted to investigate the interactions between small-molecule ligands and NDM-1. The results suggested that PSA exhibited strong binding affinity, with a remarkably low Gibbs free energy of -10.37 kcal/mol. Structural analysis demonstrated that PSA was predicted to coordinated with the Zn²⁺ active center of NDM-1 and formed multiple molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces, with key amino acid residues (ILE35, TRP93, HIS189, and LYS211), as illustrated in Fig. 3 A and C. Furthermore, the compound displayed exceptional ligand efficiency (-2.46 kcal/mol), indicating optimal utilization of its structural features for target binding. B A 2D ligand interaction diagram for PSA with NDM-1. C Hydrogen bond interactions between PSA and the NDM-1 surface. D Molecular docking of PSA with the zinc ion active center of the LpxC enzyme The pharmacological profile of PSA adheres to Lipinski’s rule of five (molecular weight: 362.36; LogP: 3.00; number of hydrogen bond donors: 3; number of hydrogen bond acceptors: 6; and number of rotatable bonds: 5), suggesting its potential as a candidate with inhibitory activity against NDM-1. Additionally, molecular docking analysis suggested that PSA may bind to the catalytic center of the LPS synthase LpxC (Fig. 3 D). This observation raises the possibility that PSA could exert synergistic antibacterial effects through additional mechanisms. In addition to inhibiting NDM-1 enzyme activity to prevent the hydrolysis of β-lactam antibiotics, PSA may potentially interact with LpxC, which could contribute to disruption of the bacterial outer membrane. Such an effect might facilitate the penetration of β-lactam antibiotics into bacterial cells, thereby enhancing their therapeutic efficacy. However, this proposed mechanism remains hypothetical and requires further experimental validation. Site-directed mutagenesis of V73A and H122A Sequencing confirmed successful insertion of the bla NDM−1 sequence between the EcoRI and XhoI restriction sites in the mutant plasmid. Specific double mutations were introduced: V73A (GTC→GCT) and H122A (CAT→GCT). No additional mutations or deletions were detected in the remaining sequences. An enzyme inhibition assay (Fig. 9 A) revealed comparable biological activity between mutant NDM-1 and wild-type NDM-1 without PSA. However, upon PSA treatment (0-128 µM), the activity of wild-type NDM-1 significantly decreased in a dose-dependent manner ( P < 0.01), whereas the activity of mutant NDM-1 remained unaffected. Val73 and His122 mutations significantly reduced the inhibitory effect of PSA on NDM-1, indicating that these residues are key binding sites for PSA. This study confirmed that Val73/His122 plays a crucial role in maintaining the stability of the PSA-NDM-1 complex and elucidated the molecular mechanism of PSA-mediated NDM-1 inhibition through competitive active-site binding. Discussion The global spread of NDM-1-producing Enterobacteriaceae, which renders carbapenems ineffective, is a critical public health challenge [ 31 ]. In this context, our study identifies phthalylsulfacetamide (PSA) as a potent NDM-1 inhibitor capable of restoring MEM activity. The repurposing of PSA, an already approved and safe drug, offers a distinct advantage over de novo drug development by significantly accelerating translational timelines [ 32 ]. This strategy holds immediate promise for veterinary medicine, where carbapenem-resistant E. coli in livestock presents serious zoonotic risks [ 33 ]. Our findings suggest that PSA-MEM combinations can be swiftly deployed in the veterinary field to control NDM-1-positive infections, effectively mitigating the risk of resistance transfer to human populations. Our study demonstrated that PSA exhibited remarkable synergistic effects with meropenem against NDM-1-positive E. coli , reducing the MIC of meropenem by 32-fold, with a FICI of 0.156. This level of synergy surpasses those reported for other metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) inhibitors, such as aspergillomarasmine A and EDTA derivatives [ 34 ]. In addition, the in vivo efficacy of the combination was particularly striking, achieving 70% survival in lethal infection models compared with 20% in controls, accompanied by a reduced bacterial load and decreased proinflammatory cytokine levels. In summary, the potent synergistic bactericidal effect observed for the PSA and MEM combination highlights the significant potential of PSA as an MEM adjuvant. Importantly, PSA demonstrated an excellent safety profile with no observable cytotoxicity at therapeutic doses, addressing a key limitation of currently available metalloenzyme inhibitors [ 35 ]. Although PSA exhibited limited intrinsic antibacterial activity in vitro, its potential contribution—particularly in the intestinal environment where it may be converted into sulfacetamide—cannot be completely excluded and may partially contribute to the observed in vivo effects. MD simulations provided crucial insights into the structural basis of PSA inhibitory activity. The observed stable binding conformation and favorable binding free energy demonstrate the high affinity of PSA for the NDM-1 active site. Notably, our mutagenesis studies revealed Val73 and His122 as critical residues for PSA binding, with alanine substitutions at these positions resulting in reduced inhibitory efficacy. This mechanism differs significantly from that of zinc-chelating compounds such as EDTA, highlighting the unique residue-specific interactions of PSA that could lead to improved selectivity and reduced off-target effects [ 36 , 37 ]. From a structural perspective, the identification of Val73 and His122 as critical binding residues provides valuable insights for rational drug design. These residues are conserved across many NDM variants, suggesting that PSA derivatives can maintain activity against diverse NDM mutants. Future studies should explore the structure-activity relationships to optimize PSA potency while maintaining its favorable safety profile [ 38 – 40 ]. While the primary and experimentally supported mechanism of PSA involves the inhibition of NDM-1, its rapid bactericidal effect—observed within 3 h in the time-kill assays—suggests that additional mechanisms may be involved. Molecular docking analysis suggests that PSA may potentially interact with LpxC, a key enzyme involved in lipid A biosynthesis [ 41 ]. If validated, this interaction could contribute to disruption of the bacterial outer membrane and facilitate antibiotic penetration [ 42 , 43 ]. However, this proposed mechanism remains speculative and requires further experimental validation. Therefore, the potential involvement of LpxC should be interpreted with caution. Although the application prospect of PSA is broad, there are still limitations in this study that need to be addressed. First, the spectrum of pathogens, including other MBLs, such as VIM and IMP, that can be inhibited by PSA remains unknown. In addition, only a single NDM-1-positive E. coli strain was evaluated in this study. Although the results demonstrate a strong synergistic effect, validation across multiple clinical isolates is necessary to confirm the general applicability of PSA as an NDM-1 inhibitor. Another limitation is that the pharmacokinetic synergy with MEM needs optimization, as the subcutaneous administration of MEM and the oral administration of PSA may lead to mismatched tissue distribution. It is important to note that the identified optimal MEM: PSA ratio of 1:2 is based on a comparison within a predefined, clinically-relevant dose range. Future studies involving more extensive dose-ranging and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modeling are warranted to refine the optimal ratio for potential clinical application. Future studies should explore PSA derivatives with enhanced blood-brain barrier penetration for systemic infections [ 44 ]. Conclusion In conclusion, this study confirms that PSA acts as a potent and specific inhibitor of NDM-1, effectively restoring MEM susceptibility in NDM-1-positive E. coli through competitive binding to the NDM-1 active site. The PSA and MEM combination demonstrated notable synergistic effects both in vitro and in vivo, significantly reducing bacterial load, alleviating histopathological damage, and increasing survival rates. These results highlight the potential of PSA as a therapeutic adjuvant against carbapenem-resistant infections. Further clinical studies are warranted to translate these findings into practical applications.
