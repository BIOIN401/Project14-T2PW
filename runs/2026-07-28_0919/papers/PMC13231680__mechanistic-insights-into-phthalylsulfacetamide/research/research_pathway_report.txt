        section: discussion   ·   best retrieval score for this source: 0.9031
        "Discussion The global spread of NDM-1-producing Enterobacteriaceae,
         which renders carbapenems ineffective, is a critical public health
         challenge [ 31 ]. In this context, our study identifies
         phthalylsulfacetamide (PSA) as a potent NDM-1 inhibitor capable of
         restoring MEM activity. The repurposing of PSA, an already approved
         and safe drug, offers a distinct advantage over de novo drug
         development by significantly accelerating translational timelines [ 32
         ]. This strategy holds immediate promise for veterinary medicine,
         where carbapenem-resistant E. coli in livestock presents serious
         zoonotic risks [ 33 ]. Our findings suggest that PSA-MEM combinations
         can be swiftly deployed in the veterinary field to control
         NDM-1-positive infections, effectively mitigating the risk of
         resistance transfer to human populations. Our study demonstrated that
         PSA exhibited remarkable synergistic effects with meropenem against
         NDM-1-positive E. coli , reducing the MIC of meropenem by 32-fold,
         with a FICI of 0.156. This level of synergy surpasses those reported
         for other metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) inhibitors, such as
         aspergillomarasmine A and EDTA derivatives [ 34 ]. In addition, the in
         vivo efficacy of the combination was particularly striking, achieving
         70% survival in lethal infection models compared with 20% in controls,
         accompanied by a reduced bacterial load and decreased proinflammatory
         cytokine levels. In summary, the potent synergistic bactericidal
         effect observed for the PSA and MEM combination highlights the
         significant potential of PSA as an MEM adjuvant. Importantly, PSA
         demonstrated an excellent safety profile with no observable
         cytotoxicity at therapeutic doses, addressing a key limitation of
         currently available metalloenzyme inhibitors [ 35 ]. Although PSA
         exhibited limited intrinsic antibacterial activity in vitro, its
         potential contribution—particularly in the intestinal environment
         where it may be converted into sulfacetamide—cannot be completely
         excluded and may partially contribute to the observed in vivo effects.
         MD simulations provided crucial insights into the structural basis of
         PSA inhibitory activity. The observed stable binding conformation and
         favorable binding free energy demonstrate the high affinity of PSA for
         the NDM-1 active site. Notably, our mutagenesis studies revealed Val73
         and His122 as critical residues for PSA binding, with alanine
         substitutions at these positions resulting in reduced inhibitory
         efficacy. This mechanism differs significantly from that of zinc-
         chelating compounds such as EDTA, highlighting the unique residue-
         specific interactions of PSA that could lead to improved selectivity
         and reduced off-target effects [ 36 , 37 ]. From a structural
         perspective, the identification of Val73 and His122 as critical
         binding residues provides valuable insights for rational drug design.
         These residues are conserved across many NDM variants, suggesting that
         PSA derivatives can maintain activity against diverse NDM mutants.
         Future studies should explore the structure-activity relationships to
         optimize PSA potency while maintaining its favorable safety profile [
         38 – 40 ]. While the primary and experimentally supported mechanism of
         PSA involves the inhibition of NDM-1, its rapid bactericidal
         effect—observed within 3 h in the time-kill assays—suggests that
         additional mechanisms may be involved. Molecular docking analysis
         suggests that PSA may potentially interact with LpxC, a key enzyme
         involved in lipid A biosynthesis [ 41 ]. If validated, this
         interaction could contribute to disruption of the bacterial outer
         membrane and facilitate antibiotic penetration [ 42 , 43 ]. However,
         this proposed mechanism remains speculative and requires further
         experimental validation. Therefore, the potential involvement of LpxC
         should be interpreted with caution. Although the application prospect
         of PSA is broad, there are still limitations in this study that need
         to be addressed. First, the spectrum of pathogens, including other
         MBLs, such as VIM and IMP, that can be inhibited by PSA remains
         unknown. In addition, only a single NDM-1-positive E. coli strain was
         evaluated in this study. Although the results demonstrate a strong
         synergistic effect, validation across multiple clinical isolates is
         necessary to confirm the general applicability of PSA as an NDM-1
         inhibitor. Another limitation is that the pharmacokinetic synergy with
         MEM needs optimization, as the subcutaneous administration of MEM and
         the oral administration of PSA may lead to mismatched tissue
         distribution. It is important to note that the identified optimal MEM:
         PSA ratio of 1:2 is based on a comparison within a predefined,
         clinically-relevant dose range. Future studies involving more
         extensive dose-ranging and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modeling
         are warranted to refine the optimal ratio for potential clinical
         application. Future studies should explore PSA derivatives with
         enhanced blood-brain barrier penetration for systemic infections [ 44
         ]."
