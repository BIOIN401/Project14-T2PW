ABSTRACT Enterobactin, a high-affinity siderophore produced by Escherichia coli and other enteric pathogens, plays a critical role in bacterial iron acquisition and virulence. By sequestering iron from host environments, enterobactin enables bacterial survival and proliferation, even under iron-limited conditions typical of host tissues. This review explores the biosynthesis and regulation of enterobactin, highlighting its contribution to bacterial pathogenesis and immune evasion. We discuss the potential of targeting enterobactin for the development of live-attenuated vaccines, emphasizing the attenuation of virulence through genetic knockout of enterobactin biosynthesis genes (e.g. entB ). Additionally, we examine enterobactin as a promising target for novel antimicrobial strategies, including small-molecule inhibitors and siderophore-based “Trojan horse” antibiotics. Beyond medical applications, we also explore the biotechnological and environmental potential of enterobactin, such as its use in bioremediation and drug delivery systems. Finally, we identify key gaps in current research and propose future directions for harnessing enterobactin to combat bacterial infections and address global health challenges. This review underscores the multifaceted role of enterobactin in bacterial biology and its potential as a cornerstone for innovative therapeutic and biotechnological applications. However, despite its abundance in the environment, iron is often inaccessible to bacteria in host environments due to its sequestration by host proteins such as transferrin, lactoferrin, and ferritin. This iron limitation poses a significant challenge for bacterial pathogens, which must compete with the host for this vital resource to establish and maintain infections [ 3–5 ]. To overcome this challenge, bacteria have evolved sophisticated mechanisms for iron acquisition, among which siderophores – small and high-affinity iron-chelating molecules – play a central role [ 6 , 7 ]. Siderophores, such as enterobactin, are secreted by bacteria to scavenge iron from the environment [ 8 , 9 ]. Enterobactin, produced by Escherichia coli and other enteric pathogens, is one of the strongest known iron chelators, with an extraordinarily high affinity for ferric iron (Fe 3 + ) [ 10 , 11 ]. By binding iron and delivering it to bacterial cells, enterobactin enables pathogens to thrive it in iron-limited host environments, such as the mammalian gut and bloodstream [ 12 , 13 ]. This ability not only supports bacterial growth but also enhances virulence, as iron acquisition is closely linked to the expression of other virulence factors and the ability to evade host immune defenses [ 14 ]. The clinical significance of E. coli and other enteric pathogens cannot be overstated. E. coli is a leading cause of urinary tract infections, sepsis, and diarrheal diseases, particularly in developing countries [ 15 , 16 ]. Moreover, the rise of multidrug-resistant strains of E. coli and related pathogens has underscored the urgent need for novel therapeutic and preventive strategies [ 17–19 ]. Targeting virulence factors, such as enterobactin, represents a promising approach to combating these infections [ 20 , 21 ]. Unlike traditional antibiotics, which kill or inhibit bacterial growth and often drive the evolution of resistance, targeting virulence factors aims to disarm pathogens without exerting direct selective pressure for resistance. This strategy not only attenuates bacterial virulence but also preserves the host microbiome and reduces the risk of resistance development [ 20 , 22 ]. The concept of targeting virulence factors for vaccine and drug development has gained significant traction in recent years. Live-attenuated vaccines, which use weakened strains of pathogens to elicit protective immunity, are a prime example of this approach [ 23 , 24 ]. By disrupting key virulence factors, such as enterobactin, researchers can create attenuated strains that are safe yet immunogenic. Similarly, small-molecule inhibitors that block siderophore biosynthesis or function offer a novel avenue for antibiotic development [ 12 ]. These strategies hold particular promise for addressing the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance and improving outcomes for patients with bacterial infections. In this review, we explore the multifaceted role of enterobactin in bacterial iron acquisition and virulence, with a focus on its potential as a target for vaccine and drug development. We also examine the broader implications of targeting siderophore-mediated iron acquisition for combating bacterial infections and addressing global health challenges. Enterobactin biosynthesis and regulation The enterobactin biosynthesis pathway Enterobactin, a catechol-type siderophore, is synthesized through a well-characterized biosynthetic pathway involving several key genes and enzymatic steps ( Figure 1 ) [ 25 ]. The ent (enterobactin) operon in Escherichia coli comprises six genes ( entA , entB , entC , entD , entE , and entF ), each encoding an enzyme essential for the production of enterobactin. These enzymes work in concert to convert chorismate, a common precursor derived from the shikimate pathway, into the mature enterobactin molecule. Here are the 4 steps of enterobactin biosynthesis [ 11 , 26–29 ]: Figure 1. Biosynthetic pathway of enterobactin in Escherichia coli . The key steps in enterobactin production, a catechol-type siderophore, include the conversion of chorismate to isochorismate (catalyzed by EntC), formation of 2,3-dihydroxybenzoate (DHB) by EntB, activation of DHB to DHB-AMP by EntE, and final assembly of three DHB molecules into the cyclic trilactone structure of enterobactin via the EntD/EntE/EntF enzyme complex. Numbers in parentheses correspond to referenced studies. Chorismate to Isochorismate : The pathway begins with the conversion of chorismate to isochorismate, catalyzed by isochorismate synthase (EntC) . This step is the first committed step in enterobactin biosynthesis. Isochorismate to 2,3-Dihydro-2,3-Dihydroxybenzoate (DHB) : Isochorismatase (EntB) converts isochorismate into 2,3-dihydro-2,3-dihydroxybenzoate (DHB), a key intermediate in the pathway. EntB also functions as a carrier protein, binding DHB for subsequent steps. Activation of DHB : 2,3-Dihydroxybenzoate-AMP ligase (EntE) activates DHB by adenylation, forming DHB-AMP. This activated intermediate is essential for the assembly of enterobactin. Assembly of Enterobactin : The final steps involve the assembly of three DHB molecules into the cyclic trilactone structure of enterobactin. This process is catalyzed by the enterobactin synthase complex, which includes EntD, EntE, and EntF. EntD is a phosphopantetheinyl transferase that modifies EntB and EntF enabling them to function as carrier proteins. EntF is a nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) that facilitates the condensation and cyclization of DHB molecules to form enterobactin ( Figure 1 ). Regulation of enterobactin production The production of enterobactin is tightly regulated to ensure that bacteria synthesize siderophores only when iron is scarce [ 30 ]. This regulation is primarily mediated by the Ferric Uptake Regulator (Fur) protein and responsive to environmental signals, particularly iron availability ( Figure 2 ). Figure 2. Transcriptional regulation of the enterobactin ( ent operon) by the Ferric uptake regulator (Fur) in response to iron availability. (A) Schematic representation of the regulatory mechanism. Under high-iron conditions (left), iron-loaded Fur (Fe-Fur) dimerizes and binds to the Fur binding site in the promoter region of the ent operon, acting as a repressor and inhibiting transcription. Under low-iron conditions (right), the apo-form of Fur cannot bind to the DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid), resulting in derepression and active transcription of the ent operon for the biosynthesis of the siderophore enterobactin. (B) Post-transcriptional repression of enterobactin synthesis by the RyhB sRNA (small RNA) under high-iron conditions. The model depicts the dual regulatory circuit controlling enterobactin production. Under high iron, the Fe-Fur complex represses transcription of the ent operon (not shown) and activates transcription of the RyhB small RNA (sRNA). The synthesized RyhB sRNA binds with complementarity to the mRNA (messenger RNA) of key biosynthetic genes entC and entF via their target sequences. This binding leads to the inhibition of their translation and/or degradation of the mRNA transcripts, providing a crucial post-transcriptional layer of repression to prevent unnecessary siderophore production when iron is abundant. (C) Integration of oxygen sensing into the regulation of enterobactin synthesis via the Fnr (Fumarate and nitrate reduction regulator) and Fur regulators. The model illustrates how enterobactin production is coupled to oxidative stress and iron availability. The anaerobic regulator Fnr, activated under low oxygen conditions, directly represses transcription of the RyhB sRNA. This relief of RyhB-mediated repression allows for enterobactin synthesis when needed. Under high oxygen conditions, Fnr is inactivated, de-repressing RyhB transcription. Concurrently, high iron levels activate Fur, which further promotes RyhB expression and directly represses the ent operon. This creates a concerted repressive signal under high-oxygen/high-iron conditions to prevent siderophore production. Thus, the ent operon is only fully expressed under low-iron and microaerobic/anaerobic conditions. Fur protein plays a central role in regulating enterobactin production. Under iron-replete conditions, Fur binds ferrous iron (Fe 2 + ) to form a complex that represses the transcription of enterobactin biosynthesis genes ( entA-entF ) by binding to their promoter regions. However, under iron-limiting conditions, the Fur-Fe 2 + complex dissociates, relieving repression and allowing the expression of the ent operon [ 31 , 32 ]. Iron availability is the primary environmental signal regulating enterobactin production, with low-iron environments, such as host tissues or fluids, triggering the upregulation of ent genes to enhance iron acquisition [ 33 ]. Other environmental factors, such as oxidative stress and pH, can also influence enterobactin production indirectly by modulating iron availability or Fur activity [ 34 ]. Beyond transcriptional regulation, post-transcriptional mechanisms involving small regulatory RNAs (sRNAs) and RNA-binding proteins fine-tune enterobactin production. For example, the sRNA RyhB downregulates siderophore production under certain conditions by modulating the stability or translation of ent mRNA [ 35–37 ]. Evolutionary and functional insights The enterobactin biosynthesis pathway is highly conserved among enteric bacteria, reflecting its critical role in iron acquisition and survival. However, some pathogens have evolved additional siderophores (e.g. salmochelin) to evade host defenses, such as the siderophore-binding protein lipocalin-2 [ 6 , 38 ]. This evolutionary adaptation highlights the importance of siderophore diversity in bacterial pathogenesis. Role of enterobactin in bacterial virulence Enterobactin plays a pivotal role in bacterial virulence by enabling pathogens to acquire iron in the iron-limited environments of host tissues. Beyond its primary function as an iron scavenger, enterobactin also contributes to immune evasion and enhances the pathogenicity of Escherichia coli and other enteric pathogens [ 39 ]. Here we focus on the mechanisms by which enterobactin promotes bacterial virulence and present examples of its role in various pathogens. Iron scavenging in iron-limited environments In host environments, iron is tightly sequestered by proteins such as transferrin, lactoferrin, and ferritin, creating an iron-limited condition that restricts bacterial growth [ 3 , 40 ]. Enterobactin, with its extraordinarily high affinity for ferric iron (Fe 3 + ), allows bacteria to overcome this limitation. Enterobactin is secreted by bacteria into the extracellular environment, where it binds ferric iron with high affinity. The iron-bound enterobactin (ferri-enterobactin) is recognized by specific outer membrane receptors (e.g. FepA in E. coli ), which transport it into the bacterial cell [ 41 ]. Once inside the cell, iron is released from enterobactin and incorporated into bacterial proteins and enzymes, supporting growth and survival. In iron-limited host environments, such as the gut, urinary tract, and bloodstream, enterobactin provides a critical advantage to pathogens by enabling them to compete with the host for iron [ 42 ]. This ability to acquire iron is particularly important for establishing infections and sustaining bacterial populations during chronic or systemic infections. Immune evasion mechanisms Enterobactin not only facilitates iron acquisition but also contributes to immune evasion, further enhancing bacterial virulence [ 43 ]. Enterobactin can extract iron from host iron-binding proteins, such as transferrin and lactoferrin, which are part of the host’s nutritional immunity strategy. By depriving the host of iron, enterobactin weakens immune cell function, as iron is essential for the proliferation and activity of immune cells, including macrophages and T cells [ 44–46 ]. Indeed, as noted before, the host produces siderophore-binding proteins, such as lipocalin-2, to sequester enterobactin and limit bacterial iron acquisition. Some pathogens, however, have evolved modified siderophores (e.g. salmochelin) that evade lipocalin-2, highlighting the evolutionary arms race between bacteria and the host immune system [ 12 ]. Examples of enterobactin’s role in pathogens Enterobactin plays a critical role in iron acquisition and virulence across a wide range of Gram-negative bacteria. Enterobactin is essential for E. coli to acquire iron in the host environment, particularly during infections such as urinary tract infections (UTIs), sepsis, and diarrheal diseases. entB mutants (unable to produce enterobactin) are significantly attenuated [ 47 ]. Furthermore, enterobactin helps E. coli evade host nutritional immunity, such as lipocalin-2 [ 39 ]. Enterobactin is crucial for Salmonella to acquire iron during systemic infections, such as typhoid fever and gastroenteritis. Enterobactin-deficient Salmonella strains show reduced virulence and are less capable of colonizing host tissues. Salmonella also produces salmochelin, a glycosylated form of enterobactin, to evade host lipocalin-2 [ 48 ]. In Klebsiella pneumoniae enterobactin is critical for the bacterium to acquire iron during pneumonia and bloodstream infections. Enterobactin production enhances Klebsiella survival and persistence in the host [ 49 ]. Although Shigella possesses the genetic machinery to produce two siderophores, enterobactin and aerobactin, the enterobactin system is seldom utilized [ 50 ]. Enterobactin and host-pathogen interactions The interplay between enterobactin and the host immune system is a critical aspect of bacterial survival and virulence [ 39 ]. This section explores the dynamic interactions between enterobactin-mediated iron acquisition and host defenses, as well as the bacterial countermeasures that have evolved to circumvent these defenses ( Figure 3 ). The host employs powerful iron-withholding defenses, including the siderophore-binding protein lipocalin-2 (Lcn2), which sequesters enterobactin (Ent), and the iron-binding proteins transferrin and lactoferrin. Critically, the biological impact of enterobactin is determined by its iron-loading status, creating a dynamic equilibrium: apo-enterobactin (Iron-free): functions as a virulence factor by acting as a pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP), triggering pro-inflammatory signaling (e.g. IL-8 secretion) in host cells. It also acts as an immunosuppressant by modulating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway, inhibiting immune activation. Ferric-enterobactin (FeEnt, iron-bound): can paradoxically support host iron homeostasis by donating iron for mitochondrial function, illustrating a potential layer of host-siderophore metabolic cooperation. This framework reveals a complex dialogue where the same siderophore can be either pathogenic or beneficial, necessitating therapeutic strategies that target both its nutritional and immunomodulatory functions. Host defenses against siderophore-mediated iron acquisition To counteract bacterial iron scavenging, the host immune system has developed sophisticated mechanisms to limit the availability of iron, a process known as nutritional immunity [ 5 ]. One of the key players in this defense is lipocalin-2 (also known as siderocalin), a protein secreted by host cells in response to bacterial infection [ 51 ]. Lipocalin-2 binds to enterobactin with high affinity, sequestering it and preventing its uptake by bacteria. This effectively starves pathogens of iron, inhibiting their growth and reducing their virulence [ 52 , 53 ]. Lipocalin-2 is particularly effective against enterobactin due to its structural complementarity with the siderophore. However, its efficacy is not universal, as some bacteria have evolved mechanisms to evade this defense [ 54 ]. The host also employs other strategies, such as the production of lactoferrin and transferrin, which sequester free iron in bodily fluids, further limiting its availability to pathogens [ 55 , 56 ]. While enterobactin’s role in iron acquisition is well-established, recent work by Keskey et al. (2025) has uncovered its capacity to directly suppress host immunity through modulation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway [ 57 ]. The study demonstrated that enterobactin’s catechol groups structurally mimic microbial AhR ligands (e.g. tryptophan metabolites), competitively inhibiting immune activation in intestinal epithelial cells – a mechanism strictly dependent on the presence of specific gut commensals like Bacteroides spp. In murine sepsis models, this immunosuppressive activity was shown to exacerbate mortality, while prophylactic administration of AhR agonists or enterobactin-sequestering agents reduced deaths by 60%. These findings position enterobactin as a dual-function virulence factor, simultaneously hijacking host iron reserves and disabling microbiota-dependent immune surveillance. Consequently, therapeutic strategies must now account for both its metabolic and immunomodulatory functions, potentially combining siderophore inhibitors with immune potentiators like AhR ligands. The iron-pathogen interaction landscape has evolved beyond the classical “tug-of-war” paradigm into a complex metabolic dialogue with dual immunological and nutritional dimensions. While nutritional immunity through lipocalin-2 (Lcn2)-mediated siderophore sequestration remains a cornerstone of host defense [ 51–53 ], contemporary research reveals iron’s dual role as both a contested resource and a metabolic regulator of virulence. Chen & Ayres (2020) conceptualize this as a “cooperative defense system” where host iron metabolism can actively tame pathogen virulence through physiological reprogramming [ 58 ]. This complexity is exemplified by three key findings: (1) Dietary iron induces insulin resistance to increase intestinal glucose availability, suppressing Citrobacter rodentium virulence while permitting colonization [ 59 ]; (2) Ferric enterobactin (FeEnt) paradoxically supports host iron homeostasis by promoting mitochondrial iron uptake in C. elegans and mammalian cells through transporter-independent mechanisms [ 60 , 61 ]; and (3) Iron-free enterobactin acts as a pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), triggering IL-8 secretion via formyl peptide receptors and labile iron pool chelation – a response potently inhibited by Lcn2 or iron saturation [ 62 ]. These findings collectively establish enterobactin as a molecular switch whose biological impact depends on its iron-loading status and host context, giving rise to an emerging paradigm with four interconnected strategic layers: classical iron withholding through host proteins like Lcn2 and transferrin; bacterial countermeasures including stealth siderophores such as salmochelin; host-siderophore metabolic cooperation where ferric enterobactin (FeEnt) serves as an iron donor; and immunological sensing mechanisms that recognize iron-free enterobactin as a danger signal. This multilayered framework reveals how the same siderophore can function either as a virulence factor or beneficial iron source depending on its iron saturation state and the host’s physiological conditions, creating a dynamic equilibrium between pathogenic exploitation and symbiotic cooperation in iron metabolism. Therapeutic approaches must therefore navigate this duality: while FeEnt may be leveraged to treat iron deficiencies (e.g. in HIF-1-related anemia), apo-enterobactin’s proinflammatory effects warrant neutralization in inflammatory conditions. This refined paradigm highlights the need for iron-status-specific interventions targeting siderophore systems. Bacterial countermeasures: Stealth siderophores In response to host defenses like lipocalin-2, bacteria have evolved countermeasures to maintain their iron acquisition capabilities. One such strategy is the production of stealth siderophores, which are structurally modified versions of enterobactin that evade detection by the host immune system. A prominent example is salmochelin, a glycosylated derivative of enterobactin produced by pathogens such as Salmonella and Escherichia coli [ 63 ]. The addition of glucose moieties to enterobactin alters its structure, rendering it unrecognizable to lipocalin-2 and allowing bacteria to acquire iron even in the presence of host defenses [ 48 ]. In addition to salmochelin, some bacteria produce entirely distinct siderophores, such as yersiniabactin (in Klebsiella pneumoniae ) [ 64 ] which are not bound by lipocalin-2. These alternative siderophores provide a redundant iron acquisition system, ensuring bacterial survival under iron-limiting conditions imposed by the host. The ongoing evolutionary arms race between host defenses and bacterial countermeasures highlights the importance of iron acquisition in the outcome of infections. Understanding these interactions provides valuable insights into potential therapeutic targets. For instance, disrupting bacterial siderophore production or enhancing host defenses like lipocalin-2 could serve as novel strategies to combat bacterial infections. Additionally, the structural diversity of siderophores and their evasion mechanisms underscores the complexity of developing broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapies. Targeting enterobactin for vaccine development Targeting enterobactin and its associated pathways represents a promising strategy for vaccine development, as disrupting bacterial iron acquisition can significantly attenuate virulence [ 47 , 65 , 66 ]. Live-attenuated vaccines, which use weakened forms of pathogens to elicit a robust immune response, are a promising platform for targeting enterobactin. These vaccines offer several advantages, including the induction of strong and long-lasting immunity by mimicking natural infections and stimulating both humoral and cellular immune responses. Rationale for targeting enterobactin Enterobactin, as a primary siderophore in many Gram-negative bacteria, plays a critical role in iron acquisition. Disrupting enterobactin production or function can severely impair bacterial virulence, making it an attractive target for vaccine development. By targeting enterobactin, bacteria are deprived of iron, which restricts their ability to proliferate and cause disease. Furthermore, the inactivation of enterobactin biosynthesis and uptake systems reduces bacterial fitness, making pathogens more susceptible to host immune defenses. Experimental studies have demonstrated the potential of targeting enterobactin by knocking out key genes involved in its biosynthesis, such as entB . For example, E. coli strains with entB deletions exhibit reduced iron acquisition and attenuated virulence in animal models, making them promising candidates for live-attenuated vaccines [ 47 ]. Furthermore, combining enterobactin targeting with other virulence factors, such as toxins or adhesion molecules, can enhance vaccine efficacy. Precautions to ensure safety and efficacy of enterobactin-targeted live-attenuated vaccines The development of enterobactin-targeted live-attenuated vaccines requires careful implementation of multiple safety measures. Genetic stabilization through dual/triple gene deletions (e.g. entB + fepA ) minimizes reversion risks, while immunogenicity can be optimized using established dose-response curves in murine models. Environmental containment is achieved through auxotrophic markers and inducible toxin systems. For clinical translation, Phase I trials should implement rigorous monitoring protocols including serial cultivation and genome sequencing, iron homeostasis management, and horizontal gene transfer surveillance. These measures combine proven strategies from approved vaccines like Ty21a with novel approaches tailored to enterobactin-specific challenges, creating a robust framework for vaccine development that balances safety and efficacy. Enterobactin as a target for antibiotic development Enterobactin, as a critical component of bacterial iron acquisition, represents a promising target for the development of novel antibiotics. There are several studies that have used enterobactin-deficient bacterial strains to explore iron acquisition, virulence, and stress responses. In Pectobacterium atrosepticum , the ΔentA mutant showed reduced oxidative stress resistance and virulence in salicylic acid-primed plants, highlighting enterobactin’s role as a conditional virulence factor [ 67 ]. Similarly, studies with uropathogenic E. coli CFT073 demonstrated that modified enterobactin pathways could be exploited for targeted antibiotic delivery through enterobactin receptors [ 68 ]. The “Trojan Horse” strategy was further validated in antibiotic-resistant E. coli OQ866153 , where enterobactin-drug conjugates enhanced bactericidal activity by leveraging FepABCDG-mediated uptake [ 69 ]. Additionally, Serratia plymuthica V4 mutants lacking enterobactin biosynthesis (via SchF0 knockout) exhibited significant fitness defects, underscoring its essential role in bacterial survival [ 70 ]. Collectively, these studies provide robust evidence that disrupting enterobactin function impairs bacterial virulence and stress adaptation while offering novel therapeutic avenues through siderophore-targeted approaches. Strategies to inhibit enterobactin biosynthesis or function Small-molecule inhibitors targeting the enzymatic steps of enterobactin biosynthesis, such as EntB (isochorismate lyase), EntE, and EntF, can be a promising strategy for antibiotic development. Compounds that mimic the transition state of EntB-catalyzed reactions are potent inhibitors. Additionally, advances in computational modeling and virtual screening can accelerate the discovery of novel inhibitors with high specificity and efficacy. These inhibitors block enterobactin production, rendering bacteria unable to acquire iron and significantly attenuating their virulence. Using high-throughput screening and structure-based drug design, it is possible to identify compounds that disrupt these enzymes, offering a potential avenue for novel therapeutics. Another potential innovative approach involves the use of siderophore analogs conjugated to antibacterial agents, known as sideromycins. These analogs exploit bacterial iron-uptake machinery, such as enterobactin receptors, to deliver toxic payloads directly into pathogens. This “Trojan horse” strategy not only enhances drug uptake but also targets iron-starved bacteria, which are often more resistant to conventional antibiotics. Several siderophore-antibiotic conjugates have entered preclinical and clinical trials, demonstrating the potential of this approach. For instance, Cefiderocol, a siderophore-conjugated cephalosporin, has shown broad-spectrum activity against multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens by exploiting iron-uptake pathways [ 71 , 72 ]. Investigating ways to enhance host defenses, such as lipocalin-2, to sequester enterobactin and other siderophores is another strategy that can be exploited by researchers. By boosting the host’s ability to limit iron availability, it may be possible to synergize with antibiotic treatments and improve therapeutic outcomes. Strategies for Broad-Spectrum Siderophore-Targeted Therapies : The structural diversity of siderophores poses a significant challenge for antimicrobial development, but emerging strategies offer promising solutions. First, pan-siderophore inhibitors target conserved molecular features, such as the catechol-iron binding motif present in enterobactin, salmochelin, and pyoverdine. This motif is critical for high-affinity iron chelation, and by mimicking or disrupting it, inhibitors can block iron uptake across multiple bacterial species [ 73 ]. Second, engineered broad-specificity conjugates utilize hybrid scaffolds that bind multiple siderophore receptors (FepA, IroN, and FpvA) with nanomolar affinity [ 74 ]. Third, host-directed approaches bypass bacterial diversity entirely by enhancing innate defenses (e.g. lipocalin-2 induction via IL-22) or deploying iron-chelating nanoparticles that sequester iron from all siderophore types equally. These strategies are being optimized through machine learning analysis of many siderophore structures to identify universal targets, while phage-derived receptor disruptors show additional promise in preclinical models. Challenges of siderophore-targeted therapies and strategies to combat these challenges Despite the therapeutic promise of enterobactin-targeted antibiotics, the evolutionary adaptability of bacterial iron acquisition pathways poses substantial clinical hurdles. Pathogenic bacteria have developed strategies to circumvent these therapies, most notably through mutations in siderophore receptors and the activation of alternative iron-scavenging systems. For example, mutations in the fepA gene, encoding the outer membrane transporter for ferric enterobactin, have been shown to significantly hinder siderophore uptake and are recurrently identified among pathogenic Escherichia coli strains [ 75 , 76 ]. Under conditions where enterobactin biosynthesis is disrupted, such as through entB inhibition, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium compensates by upregulating salmochelin production, a glycosylated enterobactin derivative that evades host lipocalin-2 sequestration; this shift has been shown to elevate salmochelin output by up to eightfold [ 77 ]. Similarly, Klebsiella pneumoniae activates the yersiniabactin siderophore system, encoded on the high-pathogenicity island (HPI), in response to iron limitation, thereby sustaining iron uptake independently of enterobactin [ 49 ]. Collectively, these compensatory mechanisms highlight the metabolic plasticity and robustness of bacterial iron acquisition networks under selective pressure from both host immunity and therapeutic intervention. Additionally, siderophore-antibiotic conjugates must be stable in the host environment and efficiently delivered to the infection site, requiring a delicate balance between maintaining antibiotic activity and minimizing off-target effects, which remains a significant challenge in drug development. To overcome these resistance mechanisms, combination strategies show particular promise. Dual-pathway inhibition simultaneously targeting enterobactin ( entB ) and aerobactin ( iucA ) reduces bacterial loads more effectively than single-target approaches in preclinical models [ 78 ]. This can be enhanced through receptor blockade using small molecules that bind both FepA and IroN receptors, or via host-directed therapies like recombinant lipocalin-2 administration that creates an iron-restricted environment regardless of siderophore type. These multi-pronged approaches, inspired by successful antibiotic adjuvants like clavulanate-amoxicillin combinations, are now entering Phase I trials for MDR Klebsiella infections (NCT052583), representing a paradigm shift in anti-virulence therapeutics. These combination strategies, while addressing current limitations, also highlight the need for continued surveillance of bacterial adaptation mechanisms, as discussed in Section 8 . Indeed, conjugate designs achieve host stability through iron-sensitive linkers [ 73 , 79 ] (e.g. Cefiderocol’s high serum integrity [ 80 ]), targeted delivery via receptor-matched siderophores (3–5× tissue enrichment) [ 81 , 82 ], and pharmacokinetic modulation, for example by conjugating with fatty acid side chains, which prolong half-life via serum albumin hitchhiking [ 83 , 84 ]. Phase II candidates like GT-1.23 now demonstrate balanced activity/selectivity profiles, addressing historical challenges in off-target effects (Clinical Trial No. NCT04894796). Biotechnology applications Beyond its role in bacterial iron acquisition and medical applications, enterobactin and other siderophores have significant potential in biotechnology. Their unique chemical properties and ability to bind metal ions make them valuable tools for drug delivery and other industrial applications. Potential for drug delivery systems The ability of enterobactin and other siderophores to bind iron and interact with specific bacterial receptors makes them ideal candidates for targeted drug delivery systems. For example, enterobactin can be conjugated with anticancer drugs to target cancer cells, which often overexpress iron-uptake receptors, enhancing drug specificity and reducing off-target effects [ 85 , 86 ]. In this regard, targeting copper (Cu) and iron (Fe) metabolism via enterobactin and other siderophores, has emerged as a promising strategy in cancer therapy, as these metals play critical roles in cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, and survival [ 87 , 88 ]. These chelators suppress tumor growth by sequestering Cu and Fe [ 89 , 90 ]. This approach offers a novel way to disrupt cancer progression and complements existing therapies. Similarly, as noted in the previous section, enterobactin-antibiotic conjugates deliver drugs directly to bacterial cells, improving efficacy and reducing the risk of antibiotic resistance [ 68 , 69 ]. Additionally, enterobactin and other siderophores can be used to functionalize nanoparticles, enhancing their ability to deliver drugs or imaging agents to specific targets. Siderophore-coated nanoparticles, for instance, can be designed to target bacterial infections or eukaryotic cells, providing a versatile platform for theranostics – combined therapy and diagnostics [ 91 , 92 ]. Other industrial applications Siderophores have significant applications in agriculture and biomining. In agriculture, they improve plant growth by enhancing iron availability in iron-deficient soils, thereby addressing nutrient limitations and boosting crop yields [ 93 ]. Additionally, siderophores act as biocontrol agents by competing with plant pathogens for iron, effectively suppressing their growth and reducing the need for chemical pesticides [ 94 ]. In the mining industry, siderophores are utilized in biomining to extract valuable metals, such as copper and gold, from low-grade ores. Their ability to solubilize metal ions makes them valuable tools in bioleaching processes, offering an environmentally friendly alternative to traditional mining methods [ 95 ]. Future directions The study of enterobactin and its role in bacterial iron acquisition has opened new avenues for research and innovation in various fields, including microbiology, immunology, and biotechnology. While significant progress has been made, several key areas remain underexplored and hold great potential for future research. Here, three critical directions for advancing our understanding and application of enterobactin-related strategies are highlighted ( Figure 4 ). Figure 4. Future directions in enterobactin research and applications. Key emerging areas include: (1) development of live-attenuated vaccines targeting enterobactin pathways, (2) novel antibiotic strategies disrupting siderophore function (inhibitors, conjugates), (3) decoding enterobactin’s role in polymicrobial infections and biofilms, (4) biotechnological applications in bioremediation and agriculture, and (5) ethical frameworks for equitable global access and resistance monitoring. Developing More Effective Live-Attenuated Vaccines Targeting Enterobactin: Live-attenuated vaccines targeting enterobactin pathways have shown promise in preclinical studies, but further research is needed to optimize their safety, efficacy, and applicability. Designing Novel Antibiotics That Disrupt Siderophore Function: The development of antibiotics targeting enterobactin and other siderophores is a promising strategy to combat multidrug-resistant pathogens. Future research should focus on: small-molecule inhibitors, siderophore-antibiotic conjugates, combination therapies, and host-directed therapies. Exploring the Role of Enterobactin in Polymicrobial Infections : Polymicrobial infections, which involve multiple pathogens, are increasingly recognized as a major clinical challenge [ 96 ]. Emerging research has illuminated enterobactin’s complex role in polymicrobial infections, where it functions as both a virulence factor and an inter-species signaling molecule. Future studies should investigate if and how that enterobactin-producing pathogens can conquer other microbes in co-culture systems. Iron scavenging also enables unexpected cross-species interactions - Pseudomonas aeruginosa has evolved sophisticated iron piracy mechanisms to hijack enterobactin-bound iron for its own survival [ 97 ]. These competitive dynamics are particularly pronounced in biofilm environments, where enterobactin can significantly enhances the microbial biofilm formation through extracellular iron storage. The therapeutic implications of these findings are becoming increasingly clear. Combination approaches targeting multiple siderophore systems simultaneously show particular promise [ 98 ]. Future research should leverage advanced tools like microfluidics platforms that simulate infection-site iron gradients and meta-transcriptomic analysis of clinical samples to fully decode these complex interactions. This growing body of evidence positions enterobactin as both a key mediator of polymicrobial pathogenesis and a promising target for next-generation antimicrobial strategies. 4. Expanding Biotechnology and Environmental Applications: Beyond medical applications, enterobactin and other siderophores have significant potential in biotechnology and environmental science. future research should explore: bioremediation, sustainable agriculture, and industrial applications. 5. Addressing Challenges and Ethical Considerations : The advancement of enterobactin-based therapies demands proactive solutions to ethical challenges, particularly in low-resource settings where enteric infections account for about 500,000 annual deaths in children under 9 ( https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diarrhoeal-disease ). To ensure equitable access, we propose a three-tiered framework: (1) Affordable implementations, such as thermostable oral formulations and low-cost diagnostics (paper-based detection strips), which circumvent cold-chain limitations and reduce costs compared to traditional IV therapies; (2) Resistance safeguards, including mandatory genomic surveillance during trials and combination therapies (e.g. enterobactin inhibitors + zinc supplementation, high efficacy in shigellosis models) to delay resistance; and (3) Global policy integration, modeled after HIV antiretroviral programs, with tiered pricing (significant cost reduction for LMICs) and streamlined regulatory approval via WHO Prequalification and African Vaccine Regulatory Forum collaborations. Conclusion Enterobactin, a highly efficient siderophore produced by many Gram-negative bacteria, plays a critical role in bacterial iron acquisition and virulence. Its ability to scavenge iron in host environments enables pathogens to thrive and establish infections, making it a key player in host-pathogen interactions. This review has highlighted the multifaceted significance of enterobactin, from its role in bacterial survival to its potential as a target for innovative therapeutic strategies. The development of vaccines targeting enterobactin pathways, such as live-attenuated strains with disrupted siderophore biosynthesis, offers a promising approach to combatting bacterial infections. Similarly, the design of novel antibiotics that inhibit enterobactin function or exploit siderophore-mediated iron uptake provides a powerful tool against multidrug-resistant pathogens. These strategies not only attenuate bacterial virulence but also hold the potential for broad-spectrum protection against a range of enteric pathogens. Beyond its medical applications, enterobactin has broader implications for biotechnology and environmental science, including bioremediation, agriculture, and industrial processes. However, challenges such as bacterial resistance, safety concerns, and scalability must be addressed to fully realize its potential. In conclusion, enterobactin represents a critical target in the fight against bacterial infections and is a versatile molecule with wide-ranging applications. Continued research into its biology, therapeutic targeting, and environmental uses will pave the way for innovative solutions to some of the most pressing challenges in microbiology and public health.
